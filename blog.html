Notes from the studio

Music education, without the mystique.

Practical writing for parents and adult learners — how to practise, what to listen for, and how to keep a beginner interested past week three.

Practice · 7 min read · 12 November 2026

Fifteen minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday.

Every week a well-meaning parent tells us the same thing: “We couldn't practise during the week, so she did an hour yesterday.” We understand the intention completely. Unfortunately, the piano does not.

Learning a motor skill is not like revising for a history test. You cannot compress it. The fingers learn through frequency — many short, attentive repetitions spread across days — far more than through duration. Twenty minutes daily will build a reliable scale in three weeks; a weekly two-hour session will build a scale that falls apart in the exam room.

Why short sessions work

Attention has a shelf life. For a seven-year-old it is roughly eight to twelve minutes of genuine focus. Practising beyond that is mostly motion without improvement — and it teaches the child that piano is tiring rather than satisfying. A fifteen-minute session that ends while things are still going well is a gift to next week's lesson.

What a good fifteen minutes looks like

  1. Two minutes: a warm-up the student already knows — a five-finger pattern, a scale, a favourite chord progression.
  2. Eight minutes: the hardest two bars of the current piece, slowly, hands separately, then together. Not the whole piece.
  3. Three minutes: play something for pleasure — an old piece, an improvisation, a song they are learning by ear.
  4. Two minutes: pack up and write one line in the practice log about what improved.

That last step matters more than it looks. Naming an improvement gives the student evidence that practice works, which is the only motivation that lasts.

If you miss a day

Nothing happens. Begin again the next day. The habit that matters is returning, not perfection. Families who treat a missed day as a failure produce students who quit; families who treat it as Tuesday produce students who play.

We would rather see five attentive minutes every day than a heroic weekend. Consistency is the technique.
